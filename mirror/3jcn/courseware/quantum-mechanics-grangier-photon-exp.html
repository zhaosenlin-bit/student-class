Grangier Experiment: Proof of the Existence of Photons

This interactive 3D quantum physics simulation models the Grangier–Roger–Aspect photon anticorrelation experiment. A heralded photon travels to a 50/50 beam splitter and is detected at either detector D1 or detector D2. The simulation counts detector events and D1–D2 coincidences so students can explore the nonclassical behavior of single-photon light.

Key idea: an ideal single photon is detected at one output of the beam splitter or the other, not simultaneously at both outputs. Suppression of D1–D2 coincidence events is called photon anticorrelation.

Second-Order Correlation g(2)(0)

The detector statistics can be represented by the zero-delay second-order correlation. In this idealized simulation the displayed estimator is

g(2)(0) = (N0N12) / (N1N2)

Here, N0 is the number of triggered trials, N1 and N2 are the detector counts, and N12 is the number of D1–D2 coincidence events. For an ideal single-photon source in this model, N12 = 0 and therefore g(2)(0) = 0. Values below 1 indicate photon antibunching or anticorrelation and are incompatible with a simple classical description of the detected field.

How to Use the Simulation

  1. Click Start to begin producing heralded photon pairs.
  2. Watch the trigger photon travel to detector D0.
  3. Follow the partner photon to the 50/50 beam splitter.
  4. Observe whether the photon is detected by D1 or D2.
  5. Compare the D1 and D2 percentages as more events accumulate.
  6. Watch the coincidence count and g(2)(0).
  7. Use Pause/Resume to inspect the apparatus and Reset to start a new run.
  8. Drag the scene to rotate the 3D view and use the mouse wheel to zoom.

What Students Should Observe

  • Random 50/50 Beam-Splitter Outcomes

    Each signal photon is randomly transmitted or reflected by the 50/50 beam splitter. A short run may not give exactly equal counts, but the D1 and D2 percentages should tend toward 50% as the number of trials becomes large.

  • One Detection per Single Photon

    In the ideal single-photon model, one photon does not divide into two independently detectable pieces. Each event produces a detection at D1 or D2 rather than simultaneous detections at both outputs.

  • Photon Anticorrelation

    The absence of simultaneous D1–D2 detections produces a strongly suppressed coincidence count. This anticorrelation is the central nonclassical feature illustrated by the Grangier experiment.

  • Heralded Single Photons

    Detection of the trigger photon at D0 signals that its partner photon has been produced. This allows the experiment to examine the behavior of a photon associated with a known trigger event.

Why the Grangier Experiment Is Important

The Grangier experiment is an important quantum-optics demonstration of the particle-like behavior of light. A classical wave can be divided by a beam splitter, but a prepared single-photon event produces only one detector click at the two output ports. The observed suppression of coincidence detections provides experimental evidence for nonclassical single-photon behavior.

Idealizations in This Simulation

  • The beam splitter has an ideal 50/50 probability.
  • Detector efficiency is treated as 100%.
  • Dark counts and background photons are neglected.
  • Accidental coincidences are neglected.
  • Timing-window and detector dead-time effects are not modeled.
